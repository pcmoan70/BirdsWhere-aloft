Migration Aloft

European bird migration from weather radar — binned by sunrise and sunset

How to read this map

What you are seeing. 139 weather radars measure the birds flying over them — how many, how high, and in which direction — as vertical profiles. Each frame of the timeline is one solar phase (Dawn, Day, Dusk, Night I–III), keyed to local sunrise and sunset rather than the clock, so a phase means the same thing in Portugal and in Finland. Traffic (MT) counts birds crossing a 1 km line during the phase; nights dominate — most migration is nocturnal.

The radar marks. In Vector mode each radar draws arrows by compass sector: length, thickness and warm colour (dark → bright) all carry traffic volume on a log scale; direction is where the birds were heading. A dashed arrow is a radar that measured density but not direction (heading borrowed from neighbours). A small hollow ring is a radar with no data for this phase — an ordinary outage; a red cross means it has been silent a whole solar day. Flow mode blends the same measurements into an animated field between radars — blank areas simply have no radar within range. Click any radar for its full profile: traffic by phase, flight altitudes, and the sector breakdown. Clicking a teal US radar opens its own panel too — density, mean heading and the day's phases.

Weather symbols. Wind barbs use the meteorological convention — half feather 5 kt, full 10 kt, pennant 50 kt — pointing the way the wind blows FROM; shown where the migration band (strongest of ~800–1500 m) reaches 5 m/s, or at the surface via the toggle. Grey wiggly-edged regions are the leading edge of precipitation — a barrier birds will not cross; blue hatched regions mark freezing ground (a frost triggers departures). The wide weather domain (America to the Urals) is there so you can see the systems that push birds around — zoom out with −.

Particle traces are passive tracers riding the migration-band wind — where the wind alone would carry a bird. Click the map to drop a purple swarm that persists (no lifetime), to follow a release for as long as you like; right-click seeds a teal line along the American Atlantic coast, Miami to northern Labrador. They advect in lockstep with the timeline: step, scrub or press play and they fly (one phase ≈ 3.4 h of transport, dispersing as they go — seeded lines fade over days, the purple swarms do not); a still timeline is a still picture, and scrubbing backwards resets them; the Clear particle swarms button (settings) wipes them all at once. Compare a trace with the arrows to separate wind drift from the birds’ own effort.

The background wash (when Breeding-species turnover is on) is a model estimate of how the expected breeding-bird community is changing this week — one colour where species are arriving, the other where they are leaving; the ramp below the toggle names the direction. The zero-change cutoff slider leaves the middle of the scale uncoloured (default ±5% of full scale), so only the meaningful gains and losses tint the map — raise it to show only the strongest turnover.

Controls. Drag to pan, wheel or double-click to zoom, +/−/0 zoom and reset, ←/→ step phases, space plays. The phase strip under the date filters which phases the scrubber visits. Side and bottom panels collapse with their tabs; the map re-fits.

Particle traces

The 0.25° ECMWF weather, reduced to what matters for migration: wind barbs in the migration band — the strongest of the 925/850 hPa levels (~800–1500 m), shown from 5 m/s — or at the surface (10 m), toggle above; the leading edge of precipitation (a barrier birds won't cross), and where the ground is freezing (≤0 °C — blue hatched regions, distinct from grey precipitation; a frost triggers departures). Particle traces: click the map to release a purple, persistent swarm (no lifetime) that rides the migration-band wind in lockstep with the timeline: step, scrub or play the dates to advect them (one phase ≈ 3.4 h of transport; a still timeline is a still picture). Right-click seeds a line of tracers along the American Atlantic coast, Miami to northern Labrador — the Nearctic departure line (teal). Enable European departure line above and the same right-click also seeds the Atlantic-facing European seaboard, North Cape to Morocco, in amber (zoom out to watch). Night shadow shades the sunlit day side warm and the night side dark for each date/phase — because the map spans an ocean, a "night" phase falls at very different clock times in America and Europe, and the terminator makes that offset visible. US east-coast radars (NEXRAD) adds the American seaboard radars in teal (matching the Nearctic tracers); each disc grows and brightens with its traffic volume on its own uncalibrated density scale (the teal ramp in settings; each radar binned on its own local night), and they light up when the shadow reaches them. Enable Radar-driven release and the radars seed tracers along the American shoreline automatically as the timeline advances (no right-click needed): each radar releases about one tracer per 250 birds of its measured traffic, spread along its shoreline — daytime detections (mostly insects and local movement) release at a quarter of that — and each tracer draws its own size from the radar-night's spread of flight speeds (radar airspeed — ground speed minus wind — stands in for size, which sets endurance: a small passerine a few days, a wader up to two weeks), so one release carries a mix of guilds. The released tracers are drawn teal to match the US layer. The same automatic release is available for the European aloft radars: enable Radar-driven release under European radars and the Atlantic-facing seaboard (North Cape to Morocco) seeds amber tracers each phase, one per 250 birds/km of each radar's traffic. Both radar releases run independently of the Show traces toggle — turn traces off and the radar swarms keep flowing (clearing only when every release is off).

Migration routes

Streamlines of the week-average radar flow — line thickness is the mean bird flow, arrows the mean direction. Interpolated between radars and faded where none is near; density-only radars borrow a direction from neighbours. Shows the structure of the average field, not individual bird tracks.

Phases are keyed to sunrise and sunset, not the clock, so a phase means the same thing in Portugal and in Finland and a whole night sits in one record. MT is birds crossing a 1 km line during the phase. Each radar sample is sorted into a compass sector by its own heading before summing, so layers moving different ways stay separate.

A red cross marks a radar that has reported nothing for a whole solar day. A single missing phase is an ordinary outage and keeps its hollow ring — the cross means the radar has dropped out, not that it saw no birds.
